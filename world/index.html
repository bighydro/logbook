    The world

    logbook export world draws the record as one page: a globe you can scrub. The world's outline, the places you have named as anchors, your boat and your aircraft as dots moving along their own tracks, your stays as dots, your journeys as tracks, and at the bottom a time scrubber that moves all of it. It is not 3D and needs no graphics card: a canvas, one script, one file. The Library serves the same page at /world.

    logbook export world                                   # <root>/export/world/world.html, the whole record
logbook export world --out ~/World                     # ~/World/world.html
logbook export world --from 2026-06-01 --to 2026-08-31  # one summer
logbook export world --tier 1                          # where you were and how you moved, nothing you wrote
open ~/World/world.html                                # any browser, from the disk, offline
logbook export world --style chart                     # world-chart.html: the record on a period chart
logbook export world --style chart --journey trip:2026-07-10:2026-07-11   # one page of one journey

    The chart, below, draws the same record on paper: Mercator's sea chart drawn on the canvas, or your own scanned chart.

    What the page shows

    • The outline. Natural Earth's 1:110m land polygons, drawn on an orthographic globe. Drag to turn it, scroll (or + and −) to zoom down to a marina, fit frames what the record holds, globe shows the whole hemisphere. At the 1:110m scale a fjord is not water; the anchors and the tracks are as precise as the record, the coast is not.
    • The anchors. Every place in places.json, a ring the size of its radius and its name.
    • The assets. Every asset of assets.json (ADR 0018), and any other subject the record's lines name under its own id: a dot where its track puts it at the scrubber's time, the last hours of the track drawn behind it, the whole track faint. Between two fixes the dot moves along the line between them; after a silence of twelve hours (more on a thinned track) it is drawn hollow at its last fix, "not seen" until the next.
    • Your stays. Every stay of the stays reader, folded as the Day folds them: a run aboard is one stay aboard. A stay already over is a solid dot, one still to come a faint one; a night's stay is larger. Your own dot is the stay you were in at that minute, between two stays it moves from one to the next, and aboard it rides with the boat: the boat's dot is ringed and named "you aboard".
    • Your journeys. Every standing journey/v1 (and trip/v1) line, RFC 0020: a passage of the deck log along its route, a ride or a train from one end to the other, a parking as a square. One still to come is dashed, one under way is drawn as far as it has come, one done is a line.
    • The chapters. When the record holds chapter/v1 lines (RFC 0036), each standing chapter is a band on the scrubber over the days it covers, named, and the line above the globe says which chapter the scrubber is in. A chapter named after its stretch began still shows: chapters are read from the whole record.

    Above the globe: the window, the tiers, the zone, how many lines were held back, and the scrubber's time as a sentence: Fri, 10 Jul 2026 19:12 · chapter Sommer på sjøen · aboard Havbris. The scrubber takes the arrow keys (an hour; a day with Shift, or Page Up and Page Down); play runs the window in forty seconds. Times are in the record's zone (logbook.json); a browser that does not know that zone shows UTC and says so, and never another zone.

    A place's or an asset's summary

    Click an anchor or an asset (or its name in the list on the left) and its summary opens, counted from the whole window as logbook show place and show asset count:

    a place an asset
    nights the nights whose stay lay at the place (aboard, the stay the boat lay at) the nights aboard it
    journeys the journeys that start or end there: by the name the line gives, or by a position inside the place's radius the journeys whose subject it is
    photos the photos taken during one of your stays at the place the photos taken while you were aboard, a passage included
    visits / fixes the first and the last day of a stay there the first and the last day of a fix

    An asset's summary also gives its identifiers (mmsi, icao24, registration) and where it is at the scrubber's time; a place's its position and radius.

    Everything is in the page

    The page is one HTML file and fetches nothing. Inside it are three blocks: the record's JSON, the land table and the script. No URL is in the page: no font, no image, no tile server, no geocoder, no stylesheet or script from anywhere. The file carries a Content-Security-Policy (default-src 'none', the one script allowed by its SHA-256) so the browser holds it to that even if a payload carried markup (every value is escaped, and the page writes text, never markup). The Library sends the same policy as a header. The test suite reads every attribute that can hold a URL, runs the export with every socket refused, and passes the page through the synthetic check the published site is held to.

    The land table is logbook/tables/land.json: Natural Earth's 1:110m land (ne_110m_land), which is in the public domain, every polygon kept and each coordinate rounded to a hundredth of a degree and delta-encoded, 36 KB. Its cap is 48 KiB (world.LAND_BYTES). scripts/make_land.py writes it from the GeoJSON file, read from disk; nothing in the package or its build fetches it.

    The record's JSON is at most 1,000,000 bytes (world.CAP_BYTES), so the whole page stays under about 1.1 MB. A track is drawn at one fix per five minutes at most: a fix is kept when the asset has moved 100 m since the last one kept, every six hours when it has not, and at both ends of a silence. When the record does not fit, the tracks are thinned first (a fix per ten minutes, twenty, … one a day), then the stays shorter than one, three, six and twelve hours are left out, a night's never. The counts are never thinned: a summary says the same at any thinning. A window that still does not fit is refused, naming --from and --to, and nothing is written. The console says how many fixes were drawn and what was thinned.

    Every id it names is in it. A stay names its place and the asset it was aboard, a journey its asset and its places, the summaries every place and asset; before the page is written, world.validate checks each one, and a page that refers to anything it does not hold is never written.

    No coordinate is more precise than the record's own. A place is as places.json spells it, a fix and a journey's route as their line does, to five decimals at most (about a metre). A stay's centre is computed by the stays reader; it is written to the fewest decimals any of your fixes inside the stay carries, and to four at most, the precision of the stay's own id. A tracker that writes three decimals gives stays at three.

    The gate (ADR 0016)

    The record is read through the gate, a year at a time (reading.read(..., tiers)): a line whose tier is not asked for is not a stay, not a track, not a journey, not a band, not a count. The default is tiers 1 and 2: where you were and how you moved (tier 1), and the chapters and the notes that make a stay a stay (tier 2). A journey with a price is tier 3 (RFC 0020) and is in no default page; nor is health or money, which the page does not draw at any tier.

    The ceiling is the world entry of policy/crossing.json, 2 when the file does not name it. --tier 1,2,3 needs the entry raised, and prints the tier-3 warning a crossing prints; an entry lowered to 1 makes tier 1 the default and refuses --tier 1,2, naming the file:

    {"hermes": {"max_tier": 2}, "mcp": {"max_tier": 1}, "world": {"max_tier": 3}}

    You write each entry yourself; a new record names only mcp, at 1. An entry may carry an until day, as every entry may (ADR 0022); past it, nothing crosses to the page.

    The record shows it left (RFC 0011)

    Every export appends one crossing/v1 line with the destination world: the window as instants, the tiers, the counts (lines logged, crossed and held back, by tier and by kind), the ceiling in force, the head before the line, and package_sha256, the digest of the folder as a vault's is digested (<sha256 of the page> world.html, hashed). Under extra.world: the page's bytes, the JSON's, how many places, assets, stays, journeys and chapters it holds, and the thinning. Nothing else is written to the record, and that one write goes through Logbook.append. The Library's /world appends nothing: nothing leaves the machine.

    The page is a function of the record's lines and settings alone: no head, no clock. A re-run over an unchanged record writes the same bytes, and the file is not rewritten ((unchanged) in the console).

    In the Library

    logbook serve answers /world with the page export world writes by default — the same request, the same JSON, the same script — under the Library's navigation, with the policy in the header rather than in the page. /world?from=2026-07-01&to=2026-07-31 narrows it. Like every page of the record, it carries an ETag and a reload at the same head is a 304.

    The chart

    A logbook is a ship's instrument, and the record drawn on a chart is what a logbook always looked like. --style chart draws the page on paper instead of the globe, beside it in the same folder (world-chart.html), with the same scrubber, the same summaries, the same gate and the same JSON:

    logbook export world --style chart                     # Mercator's chart, drawn: no image in it
logbook export world --chart ~/Charts/oslofjord.png \
  --control-points ~/Charts/oslofjord.json \
  --chart-credit "my scan of a chart in the public domain"   # your own chart as the paper
logbook export world --style chart --journey trip:2026-07-10:2026-07-11  # one page per journey

    Two rules hold on it, whatever the paper. The chart is paper, the track is evidence. The record's JSON in a chart page is the globe's, byte for byte (the test suite compares them): every position as the record has it, every minute as the record has it. The script draws a position where the paper puts it and nowhere else. An asset's track is the straight segments between its own fixes (on Mercator's chart that is the rhumb line a navigator lays off between two fixes), never smoothed, broken at a silence, never displaced to look drawn by hand. An asset is at its last fix, never slid between two (the globe slides it; the chart does not), and its summary says that fix and when. Between two of your stays the chart draws no position for you: the record has the stays, not the way between them. A day mark is a bar across a journey at a recorded position — the first fix of each of the record's days on the journey's own asset's track (at or after the journey's start), and the journey's two ends where its line puts them — and its date is the record's day of that fix or that end, as the record's zone and day_start cut it. The export works the dates out from the record's own instants and writes them into the page: never from the page's minutes, which are rounded (a fix half a minute before midnight is on the day it was taken), and never from the browser's clock, so a browser that does not know the zone still writes the record's dates (the clock under the scrubber says UTC then, as the globe's does). A night's stay is written with its first day on the page. Nothing is fetched. The paper is drawn on the canvas or carried in the page (below); the names and dates are in the system's serif, since a bundled font would have had to be fetched to be vendored, and nothing is.

    The drawn chart

    Mercator's projection (north up, a rhumb line straight), on sepia paper: the coasts of the same land table as the globe (Natural Earth's 1:110m land, public domain), hachured on the land side, the graticule engraved at a step the zoom has room for, degrees and minutes in a graduated border (the latitude cells widen towards the pole, as Mercator's do), and a compass rose with its thirty-two rhumb lines across the chart. Your stays are pricked positions — the prick and the ring a navigator draws round a fix, a night's larger and written with its day — your journeys dashed tracks in red ink with their day marks, your assets' tracks in ink (what each had sailed by the scrubber's time dark, the rest faint) with the asset's name written along the leg that brought it to its last fix, and your named places ringed and named. The legend, folded to one line at the foot, names the chart's source. Drag to move the chart, scroll (or + and −) to zoom, fit frames the record, chart shows the world. The paper is paper: in a dark room it stays light. At 1:110m a fjord is still land; the coast is as coarse as the globe's, the record as precise as itself.

    Your own chart

    --chart FILE makes your own chart the paper: a PNG or a JPEG on your disk — a scan of a chart in the public domain, or one you own — read once, never fetched, never looked up. Three more things go with it:

    • --control-points FILE, a small JSON of three or more pixels of the image tied to positions, the pixels counted from the top-left corner of the file as you give it (x to the right, y down), best at crossings of the chart's own graticule, spread over it; a point's name (at most 80 characters) is how the corner and the console call it:
    {"points": [
  {"name": "59°40′ N 10°33′ E", "x": 212.5, "y": 1648.0, "lat": 59.6667, "lon": 10.55},
  {"name": "59°40′ N 10°45′ E", "x": 1530.0, "y": 1702.5, "lat": 59.6667, "lon": 10.75},
  {"name": "59°55′ N 10°45′ E", "x": 1612.0, "y": 252.0, "lat": 59.9167, "lon": 10.75},
  {"name": "59°55′ N 10°33′ E", "x": 296.0, "y": 198.5, "lat": 59.9167, "lon": 10.55}
]}
    • --chart-credit "…", the chart's source in your words, which the legend writes as you give it. Nothing is read from the file to credit it.

    The fit. The positions are taken to Mercator's plane, as a sea chart is drawn (on a chart of a few degrees any projection is that close to it), and the transform from the plane to the image's pixels is the simplest one your points check:

    points the transform checked
    3 affine, through them exactly no: three points fit an affine transform whatever they say. The corner and the console say unchecked
    4 or more affine by least squares: a scan, turned, scaled, sheared yes
    6 or more, when the affine one does not hold projective by least squares: a chart photographed at an angle yes

    Each point's residual is how far the fit of the other points puts it from the pixel you gave (leave one out). A fit whose worst residual is more than 1 % of the image's diagonal (chart_fit.RESIDUAL_LIMIT; 36 px on a 3000 × 2000 scan) is refused, naming the point the others place worst and listing every residual, and nothing is written or appended. One wrong point raises the others' residuals too — the point named is not always the wrong one — so check them all. A latitude mistyped by a few hundredths of a degree is caught that way, and so is a chart drawn in a projection the track cannot honestly be laid on. Refused too, whatever the residuals: points nearly in a line, points that make a mirror image of the chart (a latitude and a longitude swapped, or y measured up from the bottom edge), and a projective transform whose horizon crosses the image. A projective transform is never fitted through four or five points: it has eight parameters, so the fit of the others would pass through them exactly — a check of nothing — and bend the chart to every error of a click. A thin-plate spline is not offered for the same reason: it passes through every point whatever the point says and bends the track towards it, which the track may never be. One transform takes every position of the record to the image: the track is laid on the chart, the chart is not laid on the track.

    Where the check holds. The residuals vouch for the fit inside the control points' spread (the smallest polygon round them). Beyond it a position is placed by extrapolation, which no residual checks: a chart photographed at an angle, its points bunched in the middle, can pass the check and still put a position at its far corner well off. Spread the points over the part of the chart the record crosses; the corner and the console count the positions that lie beyond the spread.

    On the page the image is the paper (the globe's coasts are not drawn on it, nor the rose: your chart has its own), the control points are crosses with their names, and the fit sits in a corner: its kind, each point's residual in pixels and as a share of the diagonal, the limit, and how many positions lie beyond the points' spread. The console prints the fit's kind, its worst residual and the limit, how many positions lie beyond the spread, and how many fall off the chart (they are drawn where the fit puts them, on no paper; beyond a projective fit's horizon, not at all).

    Only the picture crosses. An image file carries more than its pixels: a phone's photograph of a paper chart says where and when it was taken and with what. None of it reaches the page. A PNG keeps the chunks that draw it (IHDR, PLTE, tRNS, gAMA, cHRM, sRGB, IDAT, IEND), a JPEG the segments that decode it (the frame, the tables, the scans, Adobe's colour marker): EXIF and XMP, text chunks, comments, colour profiles and anything after the image's end (a second picture, a gain map) are left out, and the console names what was. An image stored turned or mirrored (an EXIF orientation other than 1) is refused: you measured the control points on the picture as a viewer turns it, and without its EXIF the page would not. Save it upright and measure on that.

    The cap. The image is carried in the page as base64 in a data block, at most 4 MiB (chart_image.IMAGE_BYTES) and 40 megapixels (IMAGE_PIXELS), and the script makes a picture of it with createImageBitmap, so the page's policy stays default-src 'none' with its one script by its hash — no data: URL, no img-src. A PNG over the cap is downscaled by the export: by a whole factor (2, 3, …, the smallest that fits), each block of pixels averaged, written again as a PNG, lossless. Your control points stay in the pixels of the file you gave; the page draws each pixel of the smaller picture as that many of yours, so the fit is unchanged. It takes a while on a large scan: the standard library, no image library, a row at a time (some ten seconds for a six-megapixel scan where it was measured, more for one whose rows are filtered by Paeth). An interlaced PNG over the cap is refused: save it without interlacing. A JPEG is never decoded or re-encoded — the standard library has no JPEG codec — so a JPEG over the cap is refused, saying about how wide to save it; measure the points on the smaller file, or give a PNG. A whole chart page is at most 7 MiB (world_chart.PAGE_BYTES): the record's JSON (1,000,000 bytes at most), the land table, the largest image as base64 and the page around them fit under it with room, and a page that would not is refused.

    One page per journey

    --journey ID makes the page of one journey, in either style, under its own name so that no page overwrites another:

    • a trip, as logbook show trips prints it, trip:FIRST:LAST, or any day inside it (as show trip takes it): the page covers its days to the return day and draws every journey line of them. The trip is found through the same gate as the page. File: world-trip-FIRST-LAST.html (world-trip-FIRST-LAST-chart.html with --style chart).
    • one journey line, by the id of its journey/v1 (or trip/v1) line — a passage of the deck log, a ride: the page covers the local days from its at to its end and draws that line alone. One above the tiers asked for, retracted, superseded or cancelled is refused. File: world-journey-ID.html (…-chart.html).

    --journey again for another page; every page is built before any is written. --journey sets the page's days, so it does not go with --from and --to.

    What the line says. Each page appends its own crossing/v1 line, as every export does. A chart's or a journey's line carries under extra.world what the globe's does not: style, journey (the id as given), and for your own chart chart — its credit in your words, the digest, bytes and type of the image as the page carries it, how far it was downscaled, the fit's kind, its number of points, its worst residual, and how many positions fell off the chart (outside) or beyond the points' spread (beyond_spread). The globe's line of a window is as it was.

    The Library's /world stays the globe.

    What it is not

    It is not a map service: there are no tiles, and the outline is coarse on purpose. The chart is not a georeferencing tool: it lays the record on your chart, and refuses a chart it cannot lay it on honestly. It is not 3D: the globe is a projection on a canvas. It is not a reader of the record for other software: the day package and the crossing package are that. And it is not a place to publish a real record without thinking: a tier-1 page still says where you slept every night.
