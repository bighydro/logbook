    The record in a browser

    logbook serve reads the record in a browser, on this machine only.

    logbook serve                     # http://127.0.0.1:8765/
logbook serve --port 8080
logbook serve --built             # labs: answered from pages built ahead (Built ahead, below)
logbook serve --install-service   # kept running by launchd or systemd (As a service, below)

    It is a reader (ADR 0013) with another output: the pages are the commands' own readers rendered as HTML instead of text, built from the index and nothing else, and nothing is written by reading. Open the address it prints, read, close it. There is no login because there is no one to log in: the server answers this machine and no other. Four pages ask you something and write your answer, each through the command that writes the same thing: the Places page names the places you return to (Places — name this place), a journey's page names the journey (Journeys), the People page merges or tells apart who the sources saw (People), and the Day is where you reflect on the day and sign it (Reflect and Sign), star a line and say who was there (The Day — Star and With). The acts, from a terminal and on the page, are on their own page.

    The demo record (logbook demo --days 30 --seed 1 --out ~/Demo/Logbook), Wednesday 17 June: a week aboard the yacht Nordlys. Nothing in it is real.

    / is Today, the front door (below). /shelves is the front page it replaces: how many lines the record holds and the days it covers, and a link to each page; the gear links it, Every shelf, as before. /today redirects to the Day of the current date in the record's timezone (logbook.json), the date before until the record's day_start when it sets one (at 05:59 under 06:00, yesterday's Day), whatever the clock of the device that asks: the page to bookmark on a phone. /day/YYYY-MM-DD?open=1 is the Day with every toggle open, for printing or saving whole, the conversations and Everything this day held among them; the page's expand all link is that.

    Every row reads as the command prints it, because it is the same code: the grammar of a stay, a move or a night is logbook/core/day.py's and logbook/core/days.py's, a trip's is logbook/core/trips.py's, a silence's is logbook/contrib/gaps.py's. A number here points at the lines it came from as it does under --json: hover an attachment and its line ids are the tooltip.

    Today, the front door

    / is what you read at 07:00, on a phone first (RFC 0047 §4.1), in the frame every door shares: the wordmark, the four doors (Today, Life, People, Places; until their own pages are built, Life opens /days, People /people and Places /places), the Ask field (/ask, below) and the gear. On a phone the doors are a bar at the foot of the screen and Ask is at the top. Every other page of the Library carries the same header above its own body, the door it belongs to marked (the Days are Life's, a person People's, a place Places'); the body keeps its look until its own door is built.

    From the top:

    1. The date and where: the day in words, a link to its Day; where you spent the night; the weather, when the record holds it.
    2. What's open: the promises both ways that are still open and were said in the fortnight before today (in pencil: the rules read them, and they are yours to confirm; an older one is on the Promises shelf, never here), the task tool's tasks for the day (read, never run, tier 1 alone, as show plan reads them), and the replies owed. Seven at most, the oldest first, each a link to its moment; the rest is one link to the Promises shelf. Who and since when are words, never a date or a count beside a name.
    3. One memory: this day a year ago, whatever it holds; else the same date in the nearest earlier year that holds a signed reflection, a starred photo or a trip (a flight, or a night away); else no card at all. One, never a list, a link to that Day, with its first sentence in your words and its starred photo.
    4. Reflect and sign: the Day's own two forms, posting to the Day (below).

    What's open, with nothing in it, says what fills it, in one sentence. Today is rendered live, as today's Day is: the date, last night and the memory read a day or a few, each kept until the record or the day moves; the promises are the fortnight's, read on the request as today's Day reads them; the plan and the replies owed are the readings the Day's morning reads, by the same keys, read beside the page under logbook serve (until they have arrived, one sentence says so, and the next visit has them; the page's tag names what has arrived). On the demo record it answers in about 50 ms the first time and 15 ms after. A built Today (--built) is served only on the day it was built. export site writes it as today.html, as of the record's last day, with no form, no Ask and no font.

    Ask

    The field at the top of every door asks /ask?q=… (RFC 0047 §4.5, v1). The words are found as logbook search finds them (the same index table, words, phrases and prefix*, a retracted line never a hit), held to the page's tier: a page at tier 1 searches tier 1. The answer is moments, not lines: one row a day, each with the day in words (a link to its Day), where you spent that night (a link to that day on the World; a place with no name is said by the place or the city it is near, never by its coordinates), who was with you (the Day's own company, names only, each a link to their page; a name the page's tier does not read is left out) and the words that matched, your own words first, the match marked and the source (message, mail, call, note, calendar …) in pencil, each a link to its row on the Day. The answer is the closest 240 matches (more words narrow it), each day once with all its matches, newest first across twelve days a page; the pages are links. No number, date or clock touches a person's name on the page: an excerpt's fields are joined by · only where letters meet (a field that begins or ends with a figure starts a line of its own), a phone number or an address alone is left out, and an excerpt whose own words set a figure beside a name shows none of them, only its source and the link to its Day, where the words are. A hit a later line corrected says so and links the line that did; ?standing=1 leaves such hits out. Nothing asked is a field beside one sentence; nothing found is "Nothing in the record says that. Try fewer words, or a name." /search stays as it is. No model answers here: questions in your own words, with cited answers, are Ask v2. An exported site has no server to ask.

    The frame (logbook/contrib/doors.py) is one stylesheet: the owner's tokens as custom properties on :root (--paper, --surface, --line, --line-soft, --tint, --ink, --pencil, --pencil-rule, --signal, --signal-deep, --card-dark, --card-dark-text, --card-dark-muted), the same for a dark page, and the three families with their fallbacks (--serif Instrument Serif, --sans Inter, --mono JetBrains Mono). A door's own rules read the tokens with a fallback, var(--paper, #F4F1EA), and never define :root.

    The gear

    The gear is the icon at the end of the doors' header, and /gear is what it opens (RFC 0047 §4.6): the machinery, in its own words, out of the way of the doors. One card per part, each with one line of state (24 sources, 2 quiet for a week or more., last crossing: site, yesterday 06:10.), then the old shelves a link away: every shelf as before (/shelves), every promise, the Ideas shelf and the table of silences (/gaps).

    • Sources (/gear/sources): one row per source, the quiet ones first: the lines it sent since its first, the day of its last line (a link to that Day), quiet when that was a week ago or more, which adapter reads it, and the command that brings it back (logbook sync NAME for a live source, logbook add NAME <file> for an export); a source policy/import.json turns off says so instead. The machine side of /gaps and of logbook doctor sources; ?quiet=1 lists the quiet ones alone.
    • Crossings (/gear/crossings): what left this machine, the newest first: each crossing line's destination, when, the tiers that crossed, how many lines and of which kinds, the entry of policy/crossing.json it crossed under and its ceiling, and a link to the line itself. ?to=site is one destination's. The Library's own destinations, the names policy/crossing.json keeps for its readers and doors and never lets a person or a package take (site, vault, world, folder, letter, notion, mcp, dream, heard and the rest), are named; a day or a package shared with a member of your circle (share day, export crossing --to) says a member of your circle, never their name, and ?to=circle lists those.
    • Agents, the Manifest and Assets: /agents, /manifest (with /manifest/people and /org/ID) and /assets at /gear/agents, /gear/manifest, /gear/manifest/people, /gear/org/ID and /gear/assets, their contents exactly, with one line at the top back to the gear. Their forms post where they always did, and the old addresses answer as they always did.
    • The build (/gear/build): when the pages were last built (below), how many and in how long, each door and shelf with when it was built and the seconds of the slowest, the command that builds them again (logbook serve --build), and the serve service serve --status names. On a machine that never built them, one sentence: every page renders live, and logbook serve --built tries the build.

    The gear reads what the Library's gate lets through: a page that reads tier 1 lists no source whose lines are all above it, and counts a crossing's lines at the tiers it reads, naming no kind, no tier and no ceiling above them; its build page lists the doors and shelves alone. No number, date or clock stands beside a person's name on any of its pages, and its slowest pages are named by their section (/people/…), never by a person's id or a day. Its readings are the ones its parts keep (/gaps' activity, /agents' sessions, the Manifest's orgs), so the gear and then a part read once. The parts are built with every other shelf; /gear and /gear/build say how the build stands now, and are always rendered. Nothing on them writes, and an exported site has no gear.

    Three rules

    Each is enforced in logbook/contrib/serve.py and tested in tests/test_serve.py.

    This machine only. The server binds 127.0.0.1 and refuses any other host before a socket is opened: --host 0.0.0.0, ::, a LAN address, even localhost (a name, and a name may resolve to anything) exit 2 naming the one host it takes. It never resolves a name, not even its own, and it never binds anywhere else: not as a service either (As a service), whose file never carries --host. To read the record from another device, see reaching it from another device.

    Who may read it. Two readers, and nobody else: this machine, and your own phone through Tailscale Serve once logbook setup phone names it (from your phone). Never through Tailscale Funnel, never through any other proxy. Before it reads anything of the record, the server looks at who asked:

    • This machine: a request with no proxy's headers (no Tailscale-*, X-Forwarded-*, Forwarded, Via or X-Real-IP) and one Host, 127.0.0.1, [::1] or localhost with the server's own port (127.0.0.1:8765), or no Host at all. Read.
    • Your phone: a request through Tailscale Serve with one Host, the tailnet name the setting names, one Tailscale-User-Login, your login, and no Tailscale-Funnel-Request. Read, at every tier this machine reads. The login is a tailnet user, not a device: every device signed in as you reads too, so keep your login to your own devices.
    • Anything else through a proxy (another login or none, another tailnet name, two Host headers, a Funnel request, any proxy at all while no phone is set): a 403, nothing read.
    • Any other Host: a 421, nothing read. This is the website you happen to have open that points its own name at 127.0.0.1 to read your pages through your browser (DNS rebinding): it can choose the address, never the Host your browser sends, which is its own name.

    A proxy that adds none of those headers and rewrites the Host to 127.0.0.1:8765 (nginx's proxy_pass with its defaults), or a raw TCP forward, cannot be told from this machine: never put one in front of the server.

    No page can be put in another page's frame (each policy says frame-ancestors 'none', each answer X-Frame-Options: DENY), and a form posted from a frame or from another site (the browser's Sec-Fetch-Dest other than document, or its Sec-Fetch-Site other than same-origin or none) is refused with nothing written, with an Origin or without one.

    One document, nothing fetched. A page is one HTML document with one inline stylesheet. No stylesheet link, no frame, no script fetched; every link, form, image and tooltip points at the server's own paths. The one font a page names is the doors' (Today), in the header every page carries: its three faces, bundled with the package under their SIL Open Font License and served by the server itself at /font/NAME (every page's policy allows fonts from the server and nowhere else, font-src 'self'). The one image is a Day's thumbnail of a photo, served from /photo/ID out of the record's own attachment store (below): the Day's policy allows images from the server and nowhere else (img-src 'self'), every other page's allows none. A script is inline on three pages, each named by its hash in that page's Content-Security-Policy and by nothing else: a dozen lines for the Places page's three keys; on a Day when this machine can hear a recording, a score of lines for its Record button, which records with the browser's own recorder and sends the recording with the form, never by a fetch (the policy has no connect-src); and the World page's canvas, whose data are two JSON blocks a browser never runs. Every other page's policy (default-src 'none') allows no script at all. The header tells the browser what the markup already does, so a payload that carried markup could not make a page fetch from elsewhere even if the escaping here were wrong (every value is escaped). The test renders every page and checks each attribute that can hold a URL, the inline style, each script and the headers.

    Read-only, but for the forms you click. Every GET is answered from the record at its head through the readers, and the readers never write, not even policy/stays.json. The test takes the head and every file before and after serving every page and finds them the same. The writes are the Places page's form (below), a journey's name (below), the People page's review (below), the Manifest's review and an org's status (below), the Ideas shelf's review and an idea's status (below), the Day's Reflect and Sign (below) and its star and with… (below), and the week's Close the week (the week), each a POST from this machine only, each through the command's own function, one at a time, and none waits for another writer: while another command holds the record's writer lock (a sync running), every one of them answers the same way rather than leaving the browser waiting: 409, the sentence Another command is writing to the record; nothing was written. Try again in a moment., the command that holds the lock, and the form you sent kept in a form that sends it again (Try again): the words you typed in their fields, the rest as they were. Nothing is written. A recording is the one input a page cannot put back: its form asks you to choose the file again. The server keeps the head it last saw, and for that head the pages it rendered and what several pages read alike (Performance): when a sync, or a form, moves the head while it runs, the next request brings the index to the new head first, the kept pages and readings go, and every page reads the new lines, with no restart. That rebuild happens once, under a lock, however many requests arrive at that moment; they wait for it rather than each starting their own on the same file.

    Built ahead

    Labs, off by default. logbook serve renders every page as it is asked for, from the index and the record as they stand, with no limit on how long a page reads: a heavy page (People, a person, the Manifest, the World, Promises on a record of millions of lines) takes its time and then answers. logbook serve --built answers from pages built ahead of time instead, not from the record while you wait (RFC 0047 §8); it, or logbook serve --build, turns the build on for this machine, and the 06:00 close keeps it built from then on. --live is still accepted and is the default. Every door and shelf (/, /days, /trips, /journeys, /people, /places, /world, /manifest, /manifest/people, /ideas, /promises, /goals, /agents, /assets, /gaps and the gear's parts) is built, with the Days of the last fortnight and the pages those shelves link to first (60 of each kind: the people at the top of People, this year's journeys, the orgs, the ideas, the goals, the promises). A built page is the page the server would have rendered at that moment, byte for byte, with one sentence added to its footer: built 06:02.

    When it is built:

    • At the 06:00 close, once turned on. sync --scheduled --close builds the Library after the sync and the dream and before doctor, and says so in one line; the demo month's first build says library: 90 pages, 90 written, in 7.8 s. A build another process holds, or one that fails, is that one line and never fails the run. On a machine where built pages were never turned on (no pages.json in the folder below), the close builds nothing and says library: not built (built pages are off; logbook serve --built turns them on).
    • After every act on a page. Reflect, Sign, a star, with…, a name, a merge, a status, Close the week: the page the act sends you back to and the front page are dropped at once, so you see what you wrote, and a rebuild of the rest starts in the background on a thread of its own. Acts that come while it builds are one more build, not one each. An act refused because the page was older than the record (409) drops the page its form was on, so your reload is the page as it stands.
    • By hand. logbook serve --build builds now and says what it did, and which pages took longest. On a record of a hundred thousand lines or more it renders on several processes, one fewer than the machine has, four at most; LOGBOOK_BUILD_WORKERS sets how many (1: one process). A page that fails is named and the command exits 1; while another process builds it exits 75.
    • The first time. A server started with --built that finds no build starts one when it starts.

    What is never built: today's Day, whose shape moves with the hour (morning, live, evening; RFC 0043 §5), the redirect of /today, and a photo's pixels. A page no build has written (a Day a year ago, a /days window you chose) is rendered when you ask for it and kept, so the next visit is a file and the next build builds it again (the latest 500 of them). Between builds the pages are as of their build: lines a sync appended at noon are on today's Day at once and on the shelves at the next close, or after your next act.

    A build after the first renders what changed. It keeps the line it read through, and renders every door and the pages they link to again, but a Day only when a line landed on it or the day after, and a journey only when it holds such a day; the rest stay as they were built. The whole-record readings the doors share are kept beside the index and brought forward by the lines appended since, not read again: the people of the whole record (people.read_whole, behind every person's page and the people review), the promises (promises.extract), and each person's last real contact (real_contacts). Each is held to the reading of every line by its own tests, and reads the whole record again when what was appended is more than lines of later days: a resolution or a retraction, a line that corrects another, a backfill into a month already read, a changed setting, or a new version of the code. A resolution, retraction or review line, or a changed setting, also makes the next build a full one. logbook serve --build --full renders every page whatever the last build left, and is never needed in normal use. On a sealed record these readings, and the review pages' tables of observed people, orgs, ideas, assets and places, are kept in the process's memory for its life instead, never on this disk, unless plaintext_cache is on (sealing): a server reads the whole record once after it starts.

    Where: library/ beside the index, in the cache folder (~/Library/Caches/logbook/<owner_id>/ on macOS, ~/.cache/logbook/<owner_id>/ on Linux, %LOCALAPPDATA%\logbook\cache\<owner_id>\ on Windows), never in the record's folder. No export, vault, crossing or copy of the record carries it, and nothing in it is a line. The folder is 0700 and every file 0600: the pages hold what the Library shows, every tier of it, so only the record's user reads them. A file is named by the SHA-256 of its address, so no address becomes a path and two addresses that differ only in case are never one file. Deleting the folder loses nothing: the next build writes it again, and with no pages.json the close builds nothing.

    A sealed record builds nothing. On a sealed record (recipients in logbook.json), no built page is kept on this disk in plain text, not even one of tier 1, which still names who is who from the resolutions (tier 2): logbook serve --built renders every page as it is asked for, reads each to its end as live does, and says so when it starts; logbook serve --build refuses in one sentence (exit 2); the close says library: a sealed record keeps no built page on this disk, …; and a build left from before is removed, unless another process is building it then (the next build removes it). The pages a running server keeps are in its memory and go when it stops. To build anyway, set "plaintext_cache": true in logbook.json (off by default): the built pages, and the readings kept beside the index (above), are then on this disk in plain text, 0600, as on a record that seals nothing (sealing; RFC 0047 §15, question 11).

    How the lab keeps itself honest:

    • The rebuild renders on a site of its own. The rebuild after an act, or after the head moved, renders every page on a Site with no build, through the server's gate, as logbook serve --build does: never the built bytes kept again, never a second built hh:mm in a footer, never a page marked as asked for when nobody asked for it.
    • A page older than the record's head is served meanwhile. After day sign on the command line, or a sync, the built pages stay the answer, each footer saying when it was built, and the server starts one rebuild for that head, however many pages are asked for while it runs; the new page is served once it is written.
    • pages.json is written only under the build lock. The server takes building.lock for the moment it writes the manifest (a page kept on demand, the pages an act dropped), or writes beside its own rebuild. While another process builds (the 06:00 close), the server writes nothing there: a page kept on demand is rendered again next time. A page an act dropped stays dropped in the server's memory until a page that read past the act is written, whoever writes the old one back. Every write is a temporary file moved into place, the lock among them (linked into place whole, removed only by the one that took it).
    • A page records the tiers it was built at. pages.json names the gate each page was built through (tiers; null for every tier, the server's) and the gate of the last build; a page is served only to a request read through that same gate, a build through another gate renders every page again, and a page that names no gate (built before 1.1) is served to none until rebuilt.
    • A sealed record keeps nothing. Every write of the lab (a page kept, the manifest, a page dropped, a build at any gate, tier 1 included) refuses a sealed record without plaintext_cache, and a record sealed while a build runs keeps nothing the build wrote.

    Never hangs

    The server keeps answering, whatever a page costs and whatever else writes the record.

    • A page has a limit. A page the build does not hold is read in a pool of two: however many are asked for together, at most two are read at once, so a whole menu opened at once never holds the record in memory a dozen times over. Live, the default, a page has no limit unless --limit SECONDS gives one: a heavy page takes its time and answers. Given one, or with --built (15 s unless --limit says otherwise; --limit 0 is none), a page read longer is stopped and answered with one sentence (Still reading, a 503): with --built it is being built in the background and will be here as a file in a few minutes, and every other door opens as usual; live, the sentence says the reading was stopped. A page the build holds never waits for the pool.
    • A browser that leaves stops its reading. The server looks at the connection while the page is read; when the browser closes it, the reading stops at the next line it reads, and nothing is sent. A broken pipe is a browser leaving, never a traceback in the log. A connection that sends nothing for 60 s is closed.
    • A reader never waits on a writer. A sync appends a line, then logbook.json, then the index (SPEC §3). A page read in between finds the index a line short: it reads the record up to the last line the index holds, every line of it whole, and names that head in its tag. It never rebuilds the index from under the writer.
    • A server that says nothing is restarted. serve --status tells a server that answers, one that takes the connection and says nothing for 5 s (not answering: stuck, or paused by the system), and nothing at all apart, and names the command that restarts it. The server asks itself for its icon every 30 s; two silences in a row, and it writes every thread's stack to its log and exits 70, which the service manager starts again (launchd's KeepAlive, systemd's Restart=on-failure). A process the system paused runs nothing, its watchdog included: the scheduled run (sync --scheduled, four times a day) restarts it, launchctl kickstart -k gui/<uid>/org.logbook.serve or systemctl --user restart logbook-serve, in one line. A system-domain service is restarted by an administrator, and the line says how.
    • When it does hang anyway, kill -USR1 <pid> writes every thread's stack to the log (~/Library/Logs/logbook-serve.log on macOS, the journal on Linux) while the server runs on: send it, and the log says where it waits.

    Performance

    A Day is one day.read: one reading of the day and the day before through index.sqlite, the health lines of the two days by an indexed column, nothing swept. On the demo record a Day answers in tens of milliseconds, a month of /days in under half a second; on a record of millions of lines a Day still renders in under a second, because the index locates the day's lines and only they are read (docs/day.md, Performance). The conversations and Everything this day held come from that same reading: a synthetic day of 3,000 messages across 40 conversations, 400 mails and 200 Notion events renders in about 0.4 s into a page of 2.3 MB (tests/test_day_page_everything.py holds it under 8 MB). /days reads the window in chunks of a month, as the command does; a year is a dozen readings. The airports table is loaded once, when the server starts.

    A reload at the same head costs nothing. Every page that is the record's and nothing else (/, a Day, /days, /trips, /places, /world, the journeys, the people) carries an ETag: the first twelve characters of the head, then a digest of the request and of what else the page reads (the rest of logbook.json, places.json, assets.json, policy/, state/places-review.json, the pages received from the circle) and of the server's start; a Day's also of the day you are in, since its forms are there from that day on, and the People pages' of the record's today, since this year, the coming anniversaries and a silence move with it. It is sent with Cache-Control: no-cache, so the browser keeps the page and asks every time; when the tag it sends back is the page's, the answer is a 304 with no body and nothing is read or rendered. A sync that moves the head, a place renamed or a restart changes the tag, and the next reload is the page again. /assets and /gaps, which the clock moves (the age of a fix, a silence that runs to now), and every error carry no tag and Cache-Control: no-store, as before.

    The server keeps what the browser may keep. A page with a tag is kept by it, so a second request for it, from this browser or another, is answered without reading anything: the same tag is the same page, as the 304 already says. It keeps the last 64 pages asked for, 64 MB at most, and drops them all when the head moves. What several pages read alike is read once for the record as it stands — the head, the settings beside the chain that the tags digest, and the day — and handed to each of them until it moves (Site.shared): the people of the whole record, their last real contacts and the promises open today, for every person's page; the year's ranking and the review queue of /people; the orgs and their review queue for the Manifest, an org's page and /manifest/people; the ideas and theirs for the Ideas pages; the promises; the sessions with an agent; and the index's counts behind /gaps, whose clock is read again on every request. Requests that ask together wait for one reading.

    A journeys page is one reading of its year and a month either side, and a journey's page one reading of its days and a week either side, as show trip reads it; the People page is people over the year and the review queue's table, built once per head; a person's page reads the whole record's people once, and every other person's page is read from that. On the four synthetic years of the tests each answers in about a fifth of a second.

    What a page reads, it reads through index.sqlite, and never sweeps the files. A night is found by bisection over the owner's stays (stays.Nights), not by reading every stay of the record once a day; the people review compares an observed person only with the people who share an address, a number or a word of a name with them; the promises and the sessions with an agent stream the messages, one line at a time, and keep only what they show. The pages that read the whole record — the People pages, the Manifest, the Promises and Agents shelves, the World, the Places review — still read it once for each head: the first of them after a sync, or after the server starts, takes the time those lines take to read (minutes, on a record of millions), and every page after it at that head, a second.

    As a service

    logbook serve --install-service                # as you: a launchd agent, or a systemd user service
logbook serve --install-service --port 8080
logbook serve --install-service --system       # a machine nobody logs in to: prints two sudo commands
logbook serve --status                         # installed? answering? (exit 0, 1, 3)
logbook serve --uninstall-service [--system]

    --install-service keeps logbook serve running, so the pages are there in the morning without a terminal open. The service runs the Python that installed it (sys.executable -m logbook.cli serve --port N, no PATH assumed) on the record this command found (LOGBOOK_HOME, and LOGBOOK_IDENTITY_FILE when it is set, so a sealed record's pages open as they do by hand: paths, never a key). It starts at load, is started again whenever it fails, and its output goes beside the sync log. As with the schedule (docs/schedule.md), the plist or unit is printed before it is written and nothing is written outside the one directory; the file comes from the same renderer as the schedule's.

    The record's home. The machine that keeps the record (ADR 0022) is often run by a dedicated user that never has a login session, and an agent cannot load in a session that never starts. That is what --system is for: run as that user, it writes the daemon to the home directory and prints the two commands that install it, which you run as an administrator. It never runs sudo itself, and it refuses to run as root (the daemon would run as root, with root's home):

then http://127.0.0.1:8765/ (/today is today's page); its output: ~/Library/Logs/logbook-serve.log; …

    Is it up? logbook serve --status lists the serve services installed here, the user's and the system's, the port each runs on, and whether a logbook server answers there: exit 0 when every one answers, 1 when one does not (with where its output goes), 3 when none is installed. The question is one HEAD /favicon.ico to 127.0.0.1, which reads nothing of the record. logbook doctor asks the same in its serve line: a pass when no service is installed or when it answers, a warn when it is installed and silent, or when two share one port.

    After a sync nothing needs restarting: the next page reads the new head (see Read-only above), and a phone's reload of a page whose head has not moved is a 304.

    Reaching it from another device

    The server binds 127.0.0.1 and nothing else, as a service as by hand; no flag changes that. Reading the pages from a phone or another machine is your business, done on this machine's terms and by a tool you choose:

    • an SSH tunnel from the other device: ssh -L 8765:127.0.0.1:8765 <this machine>, then http://127.0.0.1:8765/ there. The same port on both ends: the browser names the port it opened in its Host, and the server reads only for its own (ssh -L 8766:127.0.0.1:8765 is a 421);
    • Tailscale Serve on this same machine, tailscale serve --bg 8765, with logbook setup phone naming the phone and your login (from your phone): the pages over the tailnet only, with its certificate, to that one login.

    Nothing else reads them. Another proxy (Caddy, nginx, a tunnel service, Tailscale Serve without the setting, Tailscale Funnel ever) is refused, a 403 with nothing read (who may read it). The pages carry every tier the record holds (SPEC §4): the server reads them all because nothing it serves leaves the machine. Keep the tailnet to your own devices.

    From your phone

    Off by default. With one setting, behind Tailscale Serve, the phone reads every page and writes too: Reflect, With, Star and Sign on the Day, and the shelves' forms, each as from this machine. Without it, the phone reads nothing: every request through a proxy is a 403.

    On the machine that serves the record (the Air):

    tailscale serve --bg 8765        # https://server.tail1234.ts.net/ on your tailnet → http://127.0.0.1:8765
logbook setup phone --origin https://server.tail1234.ts.net --login ines@example.org

    tailscale serve --bg 8765 puts the Library behind HTTPS on your tailnet, with the machine's own certificate, and keeps it there across restarts; tailscaled forwards each request to 127.0.0.1:8765, so it reaches the server from loopback. It is not tailscale funnel, which would put the pages on the internet: never funnel this port. The origin is the address the phone opens, https:// and the machine's tailnet name, nothing after it. The login is yours on the tailnet: tailscale status on the Air lists each device with the login it is signed in as (or tailscale whois with the phone's tailnet address, or the admin console's Users page). setup phone prints what it will write to logbook.json, asks for a yes typed at a terminal, and writes nothing else; the server reads it with each request, so no restart. logbook setup phone alone says what is set, --off removes it, and logbook doctor names the origin on its phone line (the login shortened). Before you travel, star a line from the phone at home: a 303 and the star.

    What it allows: a page read when tailscaled forwards it from your phone with one Host, the setting's host, one Tailscale-User-Login, your login, and no Tailscale-Funnel-Request; and a POST that tailscaled forwards from your phone, all at once: from loopback, with the browser's Origin exactly the setting's (the host in any case, :443 written or not), the Host the setting's host, and Tailscale-User-Login, the header Tailscale Serve sets from the device's tailnet identity, exactly your login, each header sent once, and not from inside a frame (Sec-Fetch-Dest, when the browser sends it, is document). Then the busy 409, one write at a time, the CLI's own functions, the same headers: everything as from this machine.

    What stays refused, a 403 with nothing written: any other device or user on the tailnet (another login, or none: a tagged device and a Funnel request carry no login), another tailnet name, http://, another port, Origin: null, a page on another site in the phone's browser (its own origin), a form posted from a frame. Without the setting, the phone's forms are refused as they always were, and the refusal says what would allow them.

    A program on the tailnet is refused too. Behind Tailscale Serve every request arrives from loopback, so the server tells this machine's own requests from a proxy's by what the proxy adds: a request is proxied when its Host is not 127.0.0.1 or [::1] (any port; localhost is a name, not one of them) or when it carries any Tailscale-* or X-Forwarded-* header. A proxied POST is answered only as the phone's, through the checks above, and never because it carries no Origin: curl from any device on the tailnet, with no Origin or a login it typed, is a 403 with nothing written, the setting on or off. Requests to http://127.0.0.1:8765 or http://[::1]:8765 from this machine are answered as before; a form posted to any other host, localhost included, is treated as coming through a proxy, so a form posted to http://localhost:8765 is refused unless it is the phone's (the page itself reads there). That rests on tailscaled sending the tailnet name as Host and adding its X-Forwarded-* headers, as Tailscale Serve does today; the star from the phone before you travel confirms the first, and a curl from another device that comes back 403 the second. Keep the tailnet name to your own devices all the same (every tier is on the pages it serves), and never tailscale funnel the port. Serve it as HTTPS, tailscale serve --bg 8765, never as a raw TCP forward (tailscale serve --tcp, or any port forward): a forward adds no header and keeps the client's own Host, so the server could not tell another device's request from this machine's. The policy's write list (the MCP door, ADR 0022) is not this and is never read here; ADR 0024 records the change.

    Places — name this place

    /places is two things. First the named places of places.json, as setup places list prints them, each with the nights the record has spent there and the last visit (from the observed places, below). Then Name this place: the review queue of logbook setup places review (places), the places you return to that have no name yet, in the same order (by nights, then hours), twenty a page with more at the foot. Each cluster shows

    • a dot on a plain canvas, drawn by the server: the spot, the radius naming it would write, and the nearest named place where it lies or at the edge in its direction, with its name and distance. No tile, no map host, nothing fetched;
    • its facts, the line the CLI prints: the coordinates, visits, nights, hours, first and last visit, the asset it was aboard, the nearest named place;
    • its evidence, folded as the CLI folds it: the addresses one source wrote along one street are one line (dawarich: "Plattformveien" (38 addresses, 217 stays)), three lines are shown, and … and N more opens the rest;
    • a field prefilled with the name the evidence weighs most (blank where no source names the spot), and three acts: Name writes the field, Skip moves to the next cluster, Not a place rejects it.

    Keys, when the cursor is not in the field: Enter names, n is not a place, Space skips; Escape leaves the field. The keys act on the cluster at the top of the window. They are the page's one script, inline; without it the buttons and links do the same.

    One path. Name and Not a place post to /places/review, and the server answers through the review engine's own accept and reject, the code setup places review --name, --accept and --reject run: places.json gets the place at the cluster's centre with its fitted radius, the record gets the naming as one note/v1 line through Logbook.append, and a rejection is one entity-review/v1 line, its cache in state/places-review.json, byte for byte what the CLI writes. There is no second implementation. After a write the head has moved, and the page you come back to is read again: the named place in the table, the queue one shorter, the next cluster scrolled to. A form from a page older than the record, naming a cluster that is gone, is refused (409) and nothing is written; so is a name the engine refuses (400: already a place, no name given), with the reason on the page.

    This machine only. This is the Library's first write act, and it is allowed because the server binds 127.0.0.1 and runs as the record's own user: whoever can reach the form can already run setup places review in a terminal here. The server holds it to that: a POST is answered only when the connection comes from this machine (the client address is loopback, 127.0.0.0/8 or ::1) and, when the browser sends an Origin header, it names 127.0.0.1 or [::1] (the server's own pages, as reached from here; not localhost, a name, and not another scheme or host). A request that came through a proxy (its Host not 127.0.0.1 or [::1], or a Tailscale-* or X-Forwarded-* header) is answered only as the owner's phone (From your phone). Anything else is a 403 and nothing is written. Every page is sent with Referrer-Policy: same-origin, so the browser names the page a form came from (Origin: http://127.0.0.1:<port>) and sends nothing to other sites (under no-referrer it would send Origin: null, and every form would be refused). Through an SSH tunnel the browser's origin is 127.0.0.1 and the form works; behind any proxy but Tailscale Serve with logbook setup phone naming the phone (From your phone) the page is not read at all. GET /places/review is a 405.

    A Day's stay without a name carries a small name this place link to the Places page at its cluster (/places?at=lat,lon, which redirects to the page the cluster is on and scrolls to it, or says there is nothing to name there when the spot is named, rejected, or was never a stay). A stay inside a named place prints its name at once, as before.

    Reflect and Sign

    At the foot of every Day (the pager's reflect and sign goes there; /today is the page to keep on a phone) are the two acts that close a day, the two logbook day reflect performs at the command line (the signed day).

    Reflect. A field for a few sentences in your words. When the day has a Wispr Flow dictation that begins with reflect and no reflection was taken from it yet, the field holds it without that word, under a line naming the dictation: a draft, and nothing is written by showing it. Reflect takes it as it stands or as you changed it, as day reflect's y (or e) does: the note keeps the dictation's id under extra.dictation and the moment you spoke as its at. Otherwise the field holds the reflection on the day, when there is one, so a word misheard is mended in place; the same words are never written twice. Reflect writes one note/v1 line, label reflection, source reflect, tier 2, through reflect.write, the function day reflect --text calls: the command's line, byte for byte. The field's lang is the language most of the day's own words are in, by the rule the dream uses to say which language to write in (dream.language_of), so a phone's keyboard and its spelling follow you.

    Or say it (optional). Where this machine can hear, a second form takes a recording: a file, or on a phone the recorder the browser opens for it. Where the browser can record (it has MediaRecorder; the page is on 127.0.0.1, or on your phone behind Tailscale Serve, since a browser lends the microphone to a secure origin only), a Record button records from the microphone and sends the recording with that form when you press it again; that is the Day's one script. The recording goes up as the form's file (multipart/form-data, at most 64 MiB, half an hour of speech even as WAV), is written to a temporary folder, heard through the path of day reflect --audio (the local whisper engine of openlogbook[transcribe], the hub offline, never fetching a model), deleted, and its words written as the reflection, extra.heard_by naming the engine and model, as the command writes it. Whether this machine can hear is asked once, when serve starts: without the extra, or without the model (logbook day reflect DAY --audio FILE --fetch-model fetches it once, then restart serve), the page says which in place of the form. Typing always works.

    The proposals. With a reflection on the day, the rules read it as day reflect does (commitments, delegations, and what became of the commitments on the page: kept, missed, dropped, carried), and each proposal is a box, numbered and worded as the command prints it. None is ticked: each is a guess until you tick it. The reflection, a sentence a line, is folded beside them, so line 6 reads.

    Sign. Sign signs the day through reflect.close, the code day reflect --sign runs: under one hold of the writer lock, the ticked delegations are written as notes, then signing.sign writes the signed-day/v1 line with the lines confirmed (every line on the page but pencil, your reflection and the delegations among them; signed day), the ticked dispositions, and the moment you clicked. It is byte for byte the line logbook day reflect DAY --sign --accept N,N writes for the same reflection and the same ticks. Nothing ticked accepts nothing. A day you signed before shows Sign again and what the new signature keeps from the standing one (its confirmed lines still on the page, its dispositions, an accepted one winning on a line they share, its note); the new line carries supersedes, as RFC 0034 has it. Afterwards the section opens with the signature as day sign prints it, and the Day's signature row says when, which line, how many lines it confirmed of the page's, and the page digest whole (page sha256 …), with , the page has changed since once a line is added to the day.

    One path, this machine only. The forms post to /day/YYYY-MM-DD/reflect (Reflect, the recording, and the evening's Dream the day so far) and /day/YYYY-MM-DD/sign (Sign, and Keep and sign below), held to the Places page's rule: a loopback client and, when the browser sends one, an Origin of 127.0.0.1 or [::1], else 403 and nothing written; a GET of either is a 405. Nothing is written without the click: reading the page offers the draft and shows the boxes, and writes nothing. A form older than the record is refused, 409 and nothing written: a Sign whose reflection is no longer the latest or whose proposals changed since the page was read, a draft that is no longer on the day. Sign with no reflection on the day is refused with the two ways on (reflect first, or logbook day sign DAY without one), a day ahead of today has no forms, and a word the command refuses (an empty reflection, a number no proposal has) is a 400 with its reason. The page never waits for another writer: while a sync holds the record it says so (409, the one answer above), keeps your words, and you click Try again. Every write in the server is one at a time.

    Not the door. The page reads policy/crossing.json for the entries that say which drafts it shows (dream, and relationship for a person's) and whether Dream the day so far is offered, never its write list: this is not the MCP door (ADR 0022), and that list may stay empty. The Library is your own Sign path, on your own machine. The Purser, the orchestrator that builds the Day page elsewhere and carries your verdict back through sign_day, remains an alternative front door, open only when you name the tool in that list (the signed day).

    The footer of every Day says No model touched this page. What a model drafted is marked as a draft. It is true because the rows are computed by the readers and the proposals by rules, and the one thing a model writes about a day, the dream's draft, is headed Draft with the model's name and every unsupported sentence marked.

    Keep and sign

    In the evening shape of a Day (today after the evening hour, or a past day not yet signed) and when a signed day is signed again, the Sign form is Keep and sign (RFC 0043 §5): what you keep of the day, answered in one form and written in one POST to /day/YYYY-MM-DD/sign, under one hold of the writer lock, before the signature. Every control is a flag of the command, and the page and the command write the same lines, byte for byte, but for the line ids and the clock:

    On the page At the command line What it writes
    a box on a sentence or a link of the pencil, numbered keep N --keep N,N a note/v1 labelled learned, decision or idea; a link's entity/v1; the photo of the day's star and its note
    revisit on beside a decision --revisit N=YYYY-MM-DD the kept decision's extra.revisit, a day after the day signed
    the day in a word --word WORD a note/v1 labelled word, one on a day
    done, tomorrow or let go on a task of the day, shown by its title alone --done T,T, --tomorrow T,T, --let-go T,T a note/v1 labelled task for each task answered
    a box on a delegation (the proposals, as above) --accept N,N (day reflect --sign) the delegation's note/v1
    Sign, Sign again logbook day reflect DAY --sign the signed-day/v1 line, the kept lines among those it confirms, never the draft

    So logbook day reflect 2026-10-20 --sign --keep 2,3,4 --revisit 4=2026-11-20 --word calm --done 1 --let-go 2 --accept 1 is the same click, and logbook day sign 2026-10-20 --keep 2,3,4 --revisit 4=2026-11-20 --word calm --done 1 --let-go 2 the same without the delegation. The numbers are the command's: logbook day DAY prints keep N at the end of each keepable row and logbook show plan DAY numbers the day's tasks. The page shows no task number: a task is its title alone, since a title may open or end with a name and a number never sits beside one, and its field carries the task's Notion id, which the server numbers as --done takes it under the lock; the lines written are the command's. A sentence of the pencil is in pencil (graphite on paper) until you keep it, then in ink with the line it became, and no box: a re-sign keeps it and never writes it twice. A task answered already says how; the day's word, once kept, stands. Nothing chosen writes nothing: a task with no answer is no line, an empty word no word. The tasks are the plan's as show plan reads them (tier 1 alone), read beside the page on logbook serve; until they are read the form says so. Through a page's tier gate (a Site that reads fewer tiers than the record holds), the pencil kept from is the draft that gate shows, as the rest of the Day reads it: a draft above the gate is neither shown nor offered, and a form that names one anyway keeps nothing of it, since keep.prepared reads the draft through the same gate. So with the day in a word, a note of tier 2: above the gate it is absent from the page, and the refusal of a second word says the day has one without quoting it.

    Every item is checked before the first line is written, by the code --keep runs (keep.prepared), and any refusal writes nothing and is a 400 in one sentence: a number the pencil does not give, an event (--accept writes those), a sentence that rests on no line, a revisit day that is not a later day or not on a kept decision, two words or a number for the word, a task that is not the day's or is answered already (named by its title). A form older than the record is a 409 with nothing written: a pencil dreamed again since the page was read (the form carries the draft's id), or the day's tasks changed (it carries their ids, digested), so an answer never goes to a task the page did not show. A head that moved while the close was read and checked is HeadMoved, before the first line.

    Dream the day so far. Today's evening opens with a button that runs logbook dream day TODAY --evening (dream.dream, under the writer lock): the draft of the day so far, written as the command writes it, comes back in pencil above the form, and nothing of it is kept until you keep it. The button is there only while the owner's dream entry in policy/crossing.json stands (the dream); without one, the page says in one sentence how to add it, and nothing of the day is sent. A page whose tier gate is below the entry's max_tier offers no button either, and says so in one sentence: it would never show the draft written. The dream holds the record's writer lock while the model answers, as the command does, and the server's other forms wait for it. An expired entry, an endpoint not set, a day with nothing to dream: the dream's own sentence, and nothing sent. A past day is dreamed whole at the command line, logbook dream day DAY.

A static site (logbook export site) shows the stars, ★ beside the starred lines, and carries no form and no list of names.

    Journeys

    A journey is a trip (ADR 0019): a run of nights away from home, derived from the nights every time and never written, its id trip:FIRST:LAST. /journeys is the trips reader grown, one year at a time (the year's reading takes a month either side, so a journey over New Year is read whole and listed once, under the year it began), the latest first. Each row:

    • the name, when you gave it one, else the dates; a link to the journey's page;
    • the nights away (and how many in transit), the return day, the route as show trips prints it, the photos dated on its days (first day to return day), the named places;
    • the people confirmed on it whom the record names, each a link to their page; someone the with module saw that no resolution line names is counted (1 not placed), a link to the People page's review, and never named here;
    • the draft of the journey (RFC 0036, unit journey, a subject naming its id), folded under its head: a proposal, each sentence with its lines, an unsupported one set apart and marked, a sentence resting on a line from outside the journey's days marked so. It follows the dream entry of the crossing policy as the Day's draft does (dream): a draft above the entry's max_tier today is not shown, and when the journey's standing draft is one, the row says superseded by policy and why, its line on hover, and nothing of its text;
    • the evidence, folded: each run of nights at one place with the lines of its stays, the flights, each person with the lines that put them there, the photos, the name.

    /journeys/FIRST/LAST is one journey, read as logbook show trip reads it: the name and a field to change it, the draft folded, its stays as dots on a plain canvas drawn by the server (an equirectangular projection about their latitude, a dot per place slept at sized by its nights, the stays' numbers beside it, a scale bar; no tile, no map host, nothing fetched) and one row each with its lines, its days one row each (the night, what show days prints, a link to the Day; the evidence is the night's stay and each source's lines that day), the promises made on it (every proposal of logbook promises dated on its days, open or done, who to whom, the sentence, the due day, the line), its people confirmed and the photos.

    Name this journey. The field posts to /journeys/name, and the server names it through the function logbook add --trip runs:

    logbook add --trip trip:2026-06-15:2026-06-20 "Midsummer aboard"   # or any day of it: --trip 2026-06-16

    That is one note/v1 line in your words (whitespace folded), label name, the journey's id under extra.trip, tier 2, source manual, dated inside the journey (now, if today is one of its days, else the last minute of its last day away): the captain's naming ADR 0019 asks for, and the only part of a journey that enters the record. The latest standing name wins; a name dated on one of its days still names it after a recompute has moved its bounds. A name is never read out of a note's prose: only the label and the id make a note a name. A form from a page older than the record, naming a journey that is no longer one, is a 409; a blank name a 400; nothing written either way.

    • This chapter: the chapter today is in, since when, and its rules in your words; the first sentence of a model's draft of it in pencil, when the dream entry allows one. With none: the one command that names one, logbook chapter open "NAME" --from YYYY-MM-DD.
    • Goals for the year, then for life: each in your words, with the day you last reviewed it (or since when it holds), and Next look when a standing review names a day to look again (its extra.revisit). Never a percentage, a score or a streak.
    • The timeline, between the bounds of your life: life in logbook.json (settings), else the record's first day to the end of next year, never to the last line the record holds.     • Volumes: the years of the record as spines, this one being written, each a link to its days.

    Through a gate (export site --tier 1, a page read at tier 1), Life shows no chapter, goal, name or reflection, each your own words at tier 2, and says so; the trips, the signed days and the silences are read at the gate's tiers. It reads no track over the whole record: the Year reads its year as /journeys does, and shares that reading with it; the Life zoom reads the marks the index hands out by kind and the days kept beside the index (the reading /gaps keeps).

    People

    /people is the People door (RFC 0047 §4.3): who is in your life, and where you stand. The orbit draws you at the centre and the people the record names on four rings: Home (someone who spent at least 30 nights of the year with you at a place places.json calls home), Family (someone who carries your family name as the record spells you: policy/owner.json's names, your own resolution lines' labels, an address of yours that reads given.family@), Friends (a call, a message or a night together this year) and Everyone else. The record holds no "family" or "friend" of its own, so the ring is a rule's reading, set in pencil with the rule on hover. The order is closeness: the people you live with first, always (they sit on the innermost ring), then days together in the last year (or in the year ?year= names), then calls and messages, then how long the record has known them. It is never shown: no number stands beside a name anywhere on the door. The closest 30 are drawn; the rest are listed A to Z under "Everyone else".

    Beside the orbit (below it on a phone) is the thread of the person ?sel=ID names, or of the closest: the ring, the name, the last real contact in words ("A call, three weeks ago."), the five newest moments (today's open item, what is coming up with them, the days together, a stay away as one span, calls, entries, recordings, messages back and forth, each a link to its day), the beginning (the first day the record holds with you both, or how to give one: logbook with DAY "NAME"), the promises open both ways and two links on: your years together (on Life, once that door is built) and the places you have shared. A thread is read from the index's rows of the person's lines, never the lines.

    Under them, Heard in the last year (or in the year ?year= names) lists the people heard then, in the same order, as the People page always listed them: each name a link to their whole thread, the last real contact, and the evidence folded, uncounted. No count stands beside a name; the order says it.

    Review. Below the list is the review queue of logbook people review (people), in its order: each observed person (what one source saw: an address, a number, a spoken name) proposed against someone the record names, with the score and the evidence open, and two acts. The observed people that match nobody are folded below. This section is the only place an observed person appears on any page. Both acts post to /people/review, from this machine only; a proposal no longer in the queue is a 409.

    /people/ID is one person's whole thread: the person's page's facts, each with its evidence folded (the days together and nights under one roof counted, the last real contact and what it was, the drift); the days together as a strip over the years, one mark per day and each a link to it, then counted by year; every moment newest first; the promises open both ways, the day each was said and is due; the anniversaries of the coming month; what was said, the words of the last few messages and recordings read only when asked (?said=1, as an org's money is); the places shared; what a model drafted about them (draft/v1, unit person) in pencil, with the lines it rests on, under the relationship entry it was written under, a sentence that sets a figure beside a name held; and how the record knows them (the addresses its resolution lines bind to them). ?at=DAY marks that day's moment.

    The Manifest

    /manifest is the shelf of RFC 0037 §11, the orgs you named (review org), each under what you decided about it (logbook status org): holding (hold, add, sell), exited, passed (the anti-portfolio, each with your reason in full), let go, then no status; within a heading the most recent touch first. A touch is a line that reaches the org through one of its refs: a mail or a meeting with an address at its domain, a transcript one of its people spoke in, a page or an act on its code-host org, its group chat, its merchant. Each row is the org (a link to its page), the status and since when, its portfolio, stale when the status is one stale_days names in policy/entities.json and nothing has touched it or restated it for that long (a hold, three years), the last touch and what it was, the people on its lines and the documents, with the evidence folded: the last touch's line and the status line. It is the text of logbook show orgs (orgs read back). Above the shelf, the portfolios your orgs are in (RFC 0037 §10.2): each a link to /manifest?portfolio=…, the shelf of the orgs that portfolio holds and the people across them, as show orgs --portfolio prints it, case and spaces aside.

    Review. Below the shelf is the review queue of logbook review org (review), in its order, twenty a page: each observed org proposed against one you named, or as a new one, with its score, its hint and its evidence folded. Accept writes what review org --accept N writes, one entity/v1 line binding what the sources saw to the org; Name, for a new one, what --name N "…" writes, the org minted (its dedupe key looked up first) under the name in the field; No what --reject N writes, one entity-review/v1 line, so the pair is never proposed again. They post to /manifest/review and go through entity_review.accept and reject, the functions the command calls, so the lines are the command's byte for byte.

    /org/ID is one org (RFC 0037 §7): its refs, its status, reason and portfolio, the status form, which posts to /manifest/status and writes what logbook status org ORG DISPOSITION [--reason "…"] [--at DAY] [--portfolio "…"] writes through entity_status.write (one status/v1 line, tier 3, your reason and your portfolio on it and never a note), every touch newest first (thirty, the rest folded), the people, who introduced whom where a mail's subject says so (Intro: Ines <> Liv), the documents as references, every status line with the portfolio it carried (a retracted one struck), and the proposals that would place something with it. The ledger is read only when you ask (show the money, ?money=1): its in, out and net per year and its lines on the timeline, derived at read time from the transactions the org's merchant refs match.

    The status form's portfolio box shows the portfolio that holds the org. Left as it is, the line carries no portfolio, as the command without --portfolio; typed, it carries what you typed, as --portfolio "…"; emptied, it clears the portfolio, as --portfolio "". The form also sends what the box showed, so a page older than the record never puts back a portfolio that has moved since: a box left alone moves nothing.

    /manifest/people is the co-investor graph: everyone who appears with more than one org, the most orgs first, with the orgs and the tier of the lines that put them there; then the anti-portfolio.

    Tier 3 stays tier 3. An org's status, its reason, its portfolio and the money are tier 3. The pages read the record through the server's gate; logbook serve reads every tier, on this machine. Through a gate without tier 3 there is no status, no reason, no portfolio and no money on any of them, every org is under no status, and the shelf says how many status lines it may not show; no filter by portfolio is offered, and one asked for lists no org, the same page for every portfolio; a person who is with an org only on a tier-3 line is no edge of the graph. Such a page offers no act either, neither the queue nor the status form, and refuses both (403): a status is tier 3, and so may a no be. logbook export site has no Manifest: its pages are the Day, the days, the trips, the places, and none of them names an org's status.

    Writing, never waiting. The acts are POSTs from this machine only (a loopback client, an Origin of 127.0.0.1 when the browser sends one, else 403), one at a time, under the record's writer lock. While another command holds it the act is answered as every act of the Library is (409, the one sentence, the command that holds the lock, the form kept; above), and nothing is written (the owner's word for every act of the Library, 14 October 2026); a proposal no longer in the queue, or an org the record no longer names, is a 409 too; an answer the record refuses (a passed with no reason, a day ahead) a 400. Done, a 303 back to the page the form came from.

    Ideas

    /ideas is the Ideas shelf of RFC 0037 §11, the ideas you named (review idea), each under what you decided about it (logbook status idea): build, exploring, spark, built, killed, then no status; within a heading the most recent touch first. A touch is a line that reaches the idea through one of its refs, as review idea finds them: a Notion page under its root (a database row is its own root), an act on its repository or a link into it, a task or a call of its title. A line a bot made or an agent wrote (extra.machine), and a relations line, is never one. Each row is the idea (a link to its page), the status and since when, your reason (the latest you gave, with its status and day when a later status replaced it), stale when the status is one stale_days names and nothing has touched it or restated it for that long (an exploring, two years), the last touch and what it was, the words written this year, its repositories, how many people it was discussed with and never their names, the days since its spark and its latest titles, with the evidence folded: the last touch's line, the status line and the reason's note. It is the text of logbook show ideas (ideas read back).

Below the shelf is the review queue of logbook review idea (review), in its order, twenty a page: each observed idea proposed against one you named, or as a new one, with its score and its evidence folded. Accept writes what review idea --accept N writes; Name, for a new one, what --name N "…" writes, the idea minted (its dedupe key looked up first) under the name in the field; No what --reject N writes, one entity-review/v1 line.     /idea/ID is one idea (RFC 0037 §7): its refs, its status and reason, the status form, which posts to /ideas/status and writes what logbook status idea IDEA DISPOSITION [--reason "…"] [--at DAY] writes through entity_status.write (with a reason, first one note/v1 line in your words, then the status/v1 line naming it, both tier 2, RFC 0037 §10.5), first said, every touch newest first (thirty, the rest folded), the people it was discussed with by name (the only page that names them), its documents as references, its title thread, the words by month, every status line (a retracted one struck), and the proposals that would place something with it.

    Writing, never waiting. A page read through a gate without tier 2, an idea's status's tier, offers no act and refuses both (403). logbook export site has no Ideas.

    Phone first

    Every page is one column that works at 400 px: the navigation wraps, a row is a short head and its facts with the evidence folded under it, a day never breaks inside its hyphens, the canvas scales to the column, and on every page (the Day's Sources, the days, the trips, the places, the assets, the gaps; in the server and in an exported site) a table wider than the column scrolls inside its own box rather than the page sideways. tests/test_library_walk.py walks the pages the server serves, from / through the links on every page it reads, one page of each kind (the Day, every shelf, the Manifest, each detail page, an error, a redirect), and holds each to that column without a browser; the list of pages it must reach is read from the server's own routes, so a page added there and not walked fails it. tests/test_library_pages.py and tests/test_manifest_page.py hold the four years' widest shelves and the Manifest's pages besides, and tests/test_ideas_page.py the Ideas shelf and an idea. tests/phone_width.py parses each page with every disclosure open, applies the page's own stylesheet as a 400 px phone would (its phone breakpoint included), and fails when an element's fixed width, min-width or row of columns, with the margins, borders and padding around it, passes the column, when an image or a pre has no max-width to keep it inside, or when the stylesheet has no phone breakpoint. Its estimates err wide, and a stylesheet it cannot read fails the test rather than passing it. The Day is held to the column like every other page: its Sources table, wider than the column on some days, scrolls inside its own box.

    What it is not

    Not an editor: the pages write nothing but the namings, the reviews, an org's or an idea's status, the reflection, the notes kept at the close, the signature, the stars and the company said above, and the captain's other words — a note, a confirmation of a line — go through the CLI as lines in the record, which the next page reads. Not a web application: no framework, no build step, no state but the record, and two inline scripts, a dozen lines for three keys and a score for one button. Not the circle (ADR 0009): a page here is for the owner's eyes on the owner's machine; a shared page is a signed bundle, and that is another layer.
