# Letter 0 — why I built it

I learned to sail young, and on a boat someone always keeps the log. Not because anyone will read it that evening, but because years later somebody will ask where we were in that storm, and the log will know, and the people will not. Somewhere in my twenties I noticed my own life had no such book. It had a thousand photos, ten thousand messages, a calendar that remembered every meeting and no meaning, and a feed that remembered only what I had chosen to perform.

I am a people person. The thing I am proudest of is not anything I have built; it is the friends I have kept and the ones I pushed to start something. And yet when I tried to answer the simplest questions — how many days this year with my oldest friend, how many nights at home, who was actually on that trip — I could not. The machines knew. I didn't. The companies that held the pieces would show me an ad before they would show me my own year.

So I started collecting the log. Everything I typed was already recorded somewhere, by something, for someone else. The job was not to write it. It was to gather it once, into a book I hold, and read it back as what it was: days, with people in them. The first weeks of reading my own days back are already changing how I live them. You notice who you didn't call. You notice the week that mattered. You feel, honestly, grateful — a word I used to find soft and now think is the entire point.

I gave it away because a log you cannot take with you is not yours, and because I want it to outlive me and whatever I build next. It belongs to no one; the rules are short; two different programs must agree before anything is called final. I write under a pen name so the format can be bigger than its author.

If it works, people will keep a logbook the way they keep a passport, and hand pages of it to the people who were there. If it doesn't, I will still have the book, and so will the people I love. Either way it was worth the nights.

Record it. Recount it. Hand a page.

— bighydro
